# Production teams assign models, they rarely route them

*Research behind [`flows/04`](../../notebooks/flows/04_which_model_should_answer.ipynb), compiled
2026-10-05. Most primary sources could not be fetched while it was written, so many figures come
from search snippets; each is flagged where it appears. Check a figure against its source before
quoting it.*

Most production LLM applications do not run the per-request "model router" of the naive mental model. They choose a model per task or per agent role offline from evals and leave it fixed. They then cut cost with levers that act on tokens rather than on model choice: prompt caching, batch, context trimming, reasoning-effort settings and, for narrow high-volume tasks, distillation. The one practitioner survey that asks directly found that 44% of multi-model users choose by task type and 11% route on cost (second-hand figure). Independent 2026 benchmarks find that learned routers converge on a common plateau, recover only 7–15% of the gap to a per-query oracle, and are largely matched by a static "task type → model" table. The large 2024 router savings ("85% cheaper") came from a two-model setup with a huge price and quality gap that 2026 price sheets no longer offer. Two things are changing the conversation. Decision models such as TypeSafe's Jev (launched mid-September 2026) make the routing call itself nearly free, at about $0.00001–0.0001 and 130–390 ms per decision, which removes the old objection that a router costs as much as the call it saves. And Jevons-style demand growth means cost per task, not cost per token, is now the number that matters: agents and reasoning multiply tokens per task faster than per-token prices fall. **On sourcing:** the researchers could not fetch most primary sources (arXiv, vendor docs, OpenRouter, TypeSafe were blocked). Many figures below come from search-engine snippets of the cited pages. They are marked *(vendor claim)*, *(second-hand)* or *(unverified)* where that applies. Anthropic's pricing, effort and Claude Code cost docs, Anthropic's multi-agent engineering post, and the jevbench, NVIDIA and semantic-router GitHub repos were read in full.

## Per-request routers are a minority practice whose measured gains keep shrinking

The naive model, a component that inspects each prompt and picks the cheapest adequate model, exists as products and papers, but surveys show it is not how most teams work. LangChain's survey of more than 1,300 practitioners (late 2025) found **87% use multiple models**. Asked how they choose between them, **44% said by task type, 26% run several models and compare, and only 11% route on cost**. Cost "is no longer a critical concern" next to quality (32%) and latency (20%) as barriers ([LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/state-of-agent-engineering); split from [nocode.tech](https://www.nocode.tech/article/langchain-report-quality-not-cost-killing-ai-agents), *second-hand; only one secondary source carries the 44/26/11 split*). Enterprise surveys show the same portfolio pattern: a16z found 37% of large enterprises run five or more models, picked per job (Anthropic for coding, Gemini on cost-performance), not per request ([a16z](https://a16z.com/ai-enterprise-2025/), *via snippet*). The academic "Measuring Agents in Production" study (306 practitioners) found **70% of production agents prompt off-the-shelf models** and keep designs simple ([arXiv 2512.04123](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04123v3), *via snippet*). Vendors' own guidance is static too. OpenAI's model-selection guide says to reach accuracy with the most capable model first, then "maintain accuracy with the cheapest, fastest model possible" ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-selection), *via snippet*). Anthropic says "Choose Haiku for simple tasks, Sonnet for most production workloads, and Opus for the most complex reasoning" ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)).

The router research shows why. The 2024 foundational results were real but narrow. RouteLLM's headline, **over 85% cheaper than GPT-4 on MT-Bench at 95% of its quality**, shrinks to **45% on MMLU and 35% on GSM8K**. Those benchmarks needed in-domain data augmentation, and quality targets fell to 87–92% of GPT-4 ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/); [arXiv 2406.18665](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.18665), *numbers via snippet*). Microsoft's Hybrid LLM reported "up to 40% fewer calls to the large model, with no drop in response quality" ([ICLR 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/b47d93c99fa22ac0b377578af0a1f63a-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Both studies pitted one frontier model against a much weaker one at mid-2024 prices, the setting that gives a router the most room to work. The 2026 benchmarks use many strong, cheap candidates and are far less flattering. LLMRouterBench (33 models, 21 datasets) found that "most routing methods collapse to similar performance" and that several recent and commercial routers "fail to reliably outperform a simple baseline". **OpenRouter's router scored 24.7% below the best single model** ([arXiv 2601.07206](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07206v1)). The Routing Plateau paper (21 methods) found the best router **10–30 points below the oracle**, because routers "primarily learn global-average model performance trends rather than fine-grained, query-specific routing signals". Combined fixes added at most 2.13 points ([arXiv 2606.07587](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07587v1)). A third paper found the strongest deployable router recovers **only 7.5–14.4% of a 9.7–30.7-point oracle gap** ([arXiv 2608.08265](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08265)). A small study (294 questions) put it most bluntly. **A static per-task-type table, chosen once, fixed 21 of the 29 routable questions and scored 262/294 at $3.33 per run, against the best single model's 245/294 at $7.69** ([arXiv 2608.23023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23023)). Part of the "oracle gap" is itself sampling noise, which makes a single-draw oracle look better than it is ([arXiv 2607.03436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03436), *contents not extracted*).

Commercial routers report savings, but almost always as their own measurements. AWS Bedrock Intelligent Prompt Routing routes only within one model family. AWS reports **16% (Meta family) to 56% (Anthropic family)** average savings at about 85 ms P90 overhead ([AWS ML Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/use-amazon-bedrock-intelligent-prompt-routing-for-cost-and-latency-benefits/), *vendor-measured*). The spread tracks the price gap inside each family. Microsoft claimed its Foundry router saves "up to 60%" ([Azure](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/gpt-5-in-azure-ai-foundry-the-future-of-ai-apps-and-agents-starts-here/), *vendor claim*). A 10-prompt community test found 4.5–14.2% ([Microsoft Tech Community](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azuredevcommunityblog/optimising-ai-costs-with-microsoft-foundry-model-router/4494776), *tiny sample*). OpenRouter replaced its Not Diamond-powered predictor in August 2026 with one driven by task type and trailing community spend ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/introducing-the-new-auto-router/), *details second-hand*). That move fits the finding that coarse task type carries most of the routable signal. The most visible consumer router is a cautionary tale. ChatGPT's GPT-5 autoswitcher broke on launch day and was quickly given manual overrides. It raised free-tier reasoning use **from under 1% to 7%**, which raised serving cost, and OpenAI removed it for Free and Go users in December 2025 ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/openai-defaults-free-users-to-cheapest-model-to-cut-back-on-costs-2000700346); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/08/12/openai-gpt-5-model-router-backlash-ai-future/)). A router that escalates more often than the old default increases cost.

## What production does instead: assign by role, then attack tokens

The documented production pattern is static assignment at two granularities. Per feature, teams pick a model from evals. Inside agent systems, they tier by role. Anthropic's research system put Claude Opus 4 as lead and Sonnet 4 as sub-agents and beat single-agent Opus by **90.2%** on its internal eval. The same post reports that **agents use about 4x the tokens of chat and multi-agent systems about 15x**, and that **token usage alone explained 80% of performance variance** on BrowseComp ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Claude Code's docs tell users to default to Sonnet, reserve Opus for architecture, and set `model: haiku` for simple sub-agents. They report about **$13 per developer per active day** across enterprise deployments ([Claude Code costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)). The "Auto" modes in coding tools are the closest thing to a deployed per-request router, and they are modest. GitHub Copilot's Auto picks partly on availability and gives paid users a **10% multiplier discount** ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-12-10-auto-model-selection-is-generally-available-in-github-copilot-in-visual-studio-code/)). Cursor's Auto billing has changed several times and the sources conflict (*unverified*).

Gateways (LiteLLM, Portkey, OpenRouter) are widely used, but their "routing" is mostly reliability and governance: ordered fallback chains on 429s and outages, load balancing, per-key budgets, and spend attribution. LiteLLM's cost-triggered feature is a rule, "budget fallbacks" that downgrade a key once it exceeds its budget ([LiteLLM](https://docs.litellm.ai/docs/proxy/budget_fallbacks)). Anthropic's docs note that "several large enterprises reported using LiteLLM" to track spend by key ([Claude Code costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)). No source measured how many gateway users configure cost routing versus fallbacks. The inference that fallbacks are more common is the researchers', not a finding.

The bigger savings come from levers that never change the model. On current Anthropic list prices the gap between tiers is narrow: **Opus 5.5 at $4/$20, Sonnet 5.5 at $2/$10, Haiku 4.5 at $1/$5 per million input/output tokens**. Moving down one tier saves about 2x. **Cache reads cost 0.1x base input (0.05x on Opus 5.5)**, and batch takes **50% off both input and output**, stacking with caching ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). OpenAI and Google also discount cached input by about 90% (*secondary and doc snippets*). Agent workloads are input-heavy. Manus reports about **100:1 input to output** and calls KV-cache hit rate "the single most important metric for a production-stage AI agent" ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus), *via snippet*). At that ratio on Sonnet 5.5, a 90% cache hit rate cuts cost about **77%**, while a downgrade to Haiku cuts about 50%, and the two stack (the researchers' arithmetic, before cache-write premiums). Context trimming works by multiples: Shopify's Sidekick went from about **6,000 to 1,500 prompt tokens** with learned "gist" tokens ([letsdatascience](https://letsdatascience.com/news/shopify-says-gisting-speeds-its-sidekick-agent-with-shorter-8faad8d3), *second-hand*). Semantic caching is the weak lever. Hit rates high enough to pay come with a few percent of wrong answers, and every number found is from vendors (*unverified*).

Distillation is static assignment with a migration step: launch on a frontier model, log traffic, then fine-tune a small model once the task is stable. Checkr replaced GPT-4 with a fine-tuned Llama-3-8B for 230-way record classification. It reports **90% versus 80–82% on the hardest cases, 5x cheaper and 30x faster** ([Predibase](https://predibase.com/blog/how-checkr-streamlines-background-checks-with-fine-tuned-small-language), *vendor case study, independent press write-up at [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3541362/checkr-ditches-gpt-4-for-a-smaller-genai-model-streamlines-background-checks.html)*). OpenPipe claims 10–20x savings on repetitive tasks ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/kyle-corbitt-openpipe-fine-tuning-llms/), *vendor claim*). The 70% prompting figure above shows this remains a minority practice.

| Approach | Where the decision happens | Strongest evidence | Main cost of using it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static per-task assignment | Offline, from evals | Surveys; task-type table beat best single model at under half the cost (small study) | Re-evaluate when models change |
| Agent role tiering | Configuration | Anthropic 90.2% gain; Claude Code defaults | Multi-agent uses ~15x chat tokens |
| Predictive router | Before generation, per request | 2024 papers strong; 2026 benchmarks show plateau | 50–85 ms, retraining, opacity |
| Cascade | After cheap generation | Author-reported 50–98% on short-answer tasks | Pays the cheap call on every escalation; tail latency |
| Effort dial | Inside one model | Provider features; Snell >4x compute efficiency | Hidden thinking billed as output; cache invalidation |
| Caching / batch / trimming | Not a model choice | Provider price sheets; Manus, Shopify | Prompt discipline; batch needs latency slack |
| Distillation | Migration step | Vendor case studies (5–20x) | Data, evals, serving |
| Decision model as router | Before generation, typed probabilities | Independent jevbench; LiteLLM test | Calibration weak out of distribution |

## Cascades and effort dials decide later, or inside the model

Cascades answer the question the router cannot: "was the cheap answer good enough?" The cheap model answers first, and a signal decides whether to escalate. The reported savings are large but come from the authors' own short-answer benchmarks at 2023–24 price gaps. FrugalGPT's "up to 98% cost reduction" comes from one financial-headline classification dataset where small models nearly match GPT-4, with a trained DistilBERT scorer ([ar5iv 2305.05176](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.05176); [Portkey summary](https://portkey.ai/blog/implementing-frugalgpt-smarter-llm-usage-for-lower-costs/)). AutoMix uses self-verification plus a POMDP meta-verifier and reports more than 50% savings on context-grounded datasets. A secondary summary notes that simple verifier thresholding sometimes did worse than random mixing ([arXiv 2310.12963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12963v3); [Lacuna](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/automix-automatically-mixing-language-models/wrk_3ff0c6f00d07d929545ce7f23019eee9), *second-hand*). Online cascade learning reports up to 90% savings ([ICML 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/nie24a.html)). The ETH "cascade routing" work proves the combined route-then-cascade strategy optimal. It reports gains of up to 8% on RouterBench and 14% on SWE-Bench and names **quality estimators as "the critical factor"** ([ETH SRI](https://www.sri.inf.ethz.ch/publications/dekoninck2024cascaderouting)).

The arithmetic shows where cascades lose. With cheap cost *c_s*, verifier cost *c_v*, strong cost *c_L* and escalation rate *p*, a cascade costs *c_s + c_v + p·c_L*. It beats the strong model only when *p < 1 − (c_s + c_v)/c_L*. Against an ideal router escalating the same share, it wastes *p·c_s* plus the verifier cost, so it wins only if its post-hoc signal picks a better escalation set. Worst-case latency is the sum of both calls, and a streaming UI cannot show the cheap answer before it is checked. Cascades therefore suit batch and short checkable outputs and fit interactive chat poorly. Agreement-based signals (k samples) multiply *c_s* by k. The signal is also less trustworthy than it looks. Post-training degraded GPT-4's calibration on MMLU from **ECE 0.007 to 0.074** ([GPT-4 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.08774v4), *via snippet*). Verbalized confidence is often better calibrated than log-probs for RLHF models ([Tian et al., EMNLP 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.330/)). A July 2026 paper warns that with threshold deferral "the cascade's error rate becomes an uncontrolled random variable", and that overconfidence is worst on the reasoning-heavy queries that most need escalation ([arXiv 2607.25018](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.25018v1)).

Reasoning-effort dials move the routing decision inside one model, and every major provider ships one. OpenAI has `reasoning.effort`; GPT-5.1 made "none" the default ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/gpt-5.1), *via snippet*). Anthropic has `output_config.effort` with adaptive thinking, where "the model evaluates each request and decides for itself whether to think and how much". Google has `thinkingBudget` and `thinking_level` ([Anthropic effort docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/effort); [Firebase docs](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/thinking)). In all three, **hidden thinking is billed as output**, and Anthropic bills the full thinking even when only a summary is returned ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/thinking-steering-and-cost)). Effort is a direct cost dial. A high-effort small model can cost more per task than a low-effort large one. Anthropic recommends "dynamic effort… based on task complexity", which amounts to routing between effort levels instead of models. It also warns that changing effort between requests invalidates prompt-cache breakpoints unless a beta per-message setting is used ([Anthropic effort docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/effort)). On cached multi-turn workloads, per-query effort switching can cost more in cache rewrites than it saves. The research case for adapting effort per query is strong. Allocating test-time compute by prompt difficulty is **more than 4x more efficient than uniform best-of-N** ([Snell et al., ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf)). o1-style models spent **1,953% more tokens** than conventional models on "2+3" ([arXiv 2412.21187](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21187)). Longer reasoning lowers accuracy on some tasks ([arXiv 2507.14417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14417v1)). No source compared routing between models with routing between effort levels at equal cost.

## Jev makes the routing decision nearly free, but not reliably calibrated

TypeSafe AI launched Jev on 15 or 16 September 2026 (sources differ). It came with a **$40M seed at a $200M valuation** ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/151032-typesafe-exits-stealth-with-40m-seed-to-build-ai-for-software-not-people/), *via snippet*). Within days came reports, second-hand from The Information, of talks for **$1B+ at roughly $10B** ([cryptobriefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/typesafe-ai-billion-dollar-funding-jev-model/), *second-hand, unconfirmed negotiation*). Jev is a "System One" model. It reads a state (text or JSON) plus named typed questions (choice, score, or yes/no "noul") and returns probabilities, never prose. It costs **$0.042 per million input tokens, with free output**, and is served through OpenRouter as `typesafe/jev-1.13` on an alpha `/decisions` endpoint ([OpenRouter model page](https://openrouter.ai/typesafe/jev-1.13), *via snippet*; [cobusgreyling/Jev](https://github.com/cobusgreyling/Jev)). The repo's `cbnb/decisions.py` already wraps that endpoint. The "parallel sampler" architecture and "RLCD" calibration training are TypeSafe's own descriptions, documented nowhere independent. TypeSafe's headline, **"193.6x faster, 444.6x cheaper"**, comes from four workflow evals its own team wrote, and TypeSafe itself calls those multipliers "on the higher end of real world gains" ([remio.ai](https://www.remio.ai/post/typesafe-ai-jev-funding-puts-a-445-cost-claim-under-scrutiny), *vendor claim via snippet; not reproduced*). Hacker News critics noted the comparison pits three floats against hundreds of generated tokens.

TypeSafe pitches Jev directly at routing. "Most agents give both the same [model] because picking between them costs a call. Jev can be used as the classifier in front of an LLM router" ([width.ai](https://www.width.ai/post/what-is-jev-ai-typesafe), *via snippet*). That targets the "decision tax", the old objection that a routing call costs about as much as the cheap call it saves. Independent tests mostly support the price and latency and qualify the accuracy. The independent jevbench (n=500 per dataset, read in full) shows Jev **0.2–5.3 points below Claude Sonnet 5 on three closed classification sets** at about **1/50th to 1/80th the cost** and about 380 ms median. It beats GPT-5-mini on both accuracy and cost. A **fine-tuned DistilBERT still wins outright** on AG News and Banking77 at 6–8 ms ([jevbench](https://github.com/dhruvmehra/jevbench/blob/main/docs/results/2026-09-22-n500-summary.md)).

| Banking77 (77 intents), jevbench | Accuracy | p50 latency | $ per 1k |
|---|---|---|---|
| DistilBERT fine-tuned | 88.0 | 8 ms | ~0 |
| Claude Sonnet 5 | 77.4 | 1,995 ms | $6.43 |
| Jev 1.13 | 76.4 | 389 ms | $0.083 |
| GPT-5-mini | 73.6 | 1,312 ms | $0.38 |

The one routing-specific test used Jev as the tier classifier in LiteLLM's Auto Router. Jev matched expected tiers **95% of the time against 73.75% for Haiku**, at 127 ms against 688 ms and 96% lower classifier cost. The authors caution that the expected tiers were synthetic, unreviewed labels and that the test says nothing about final answer quality ([LiteLLM](https://docs.litellm.ai/blog/jev-auto-router-benchmark), *via snippet*). OpenRouter, which distributes Jev and is therefore an interested party, measured Jev at 81.0% against Opus 5's 84.4% on Banking77, 13x faster at about 1/22 the cost ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/insights/jev-vs-claude-opus-5-classification/), *via snippet*). Calibration, the property a confidence-gated router depends on, is good in distribution: jevbench ECE runs from 0.026 to 0.125. It degrades where it matters most. An out-of-distribution study found Jev **correct 44.7% of the time at mean probability 0.74** on a ticket question whose answer depended on policy missing from the state ([eesel.ai](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/typesafe-jev-review), *via snippet*). That is the "I can't tell, escalate" case. No independent test has measured end-to-end answer quality and cost when Jev routes between LLM tiers.

Jev is reshaping the discussion more than the evidence base. Within two weeks a category formed: Convai released an open-weight decision model (Laya, weak zero-shot), and OpenAI previewed a "Decisions API" at DevDay on 29 September 2026. OpenAI claims 150 ms against 1.6 s and frames the API as a way to "classify content, route requests, or choose an agent's next action". Schema and pricing were unpublished as of 30 September ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/30/openais-jev-clone-could-help-the-frontier-lab-stop-its-swarming-agents/), *via snippet*). The architecture being promoted is a fast typed-probability tier that gates whether, and which, generative model runs. It fits the evidence above: if task type carries most of the routable signal, a cheap zero-shot classifier whose labels can change without retraining is the right tool. It does not fix the plateau. Jev predicts a route from the prompt like any other router, and its probabilities need per-workload threshold validation.

## Jevons turns the problem into cost per task

The name Jev reportedly nods to William Stanley Jevons ([width.ai](https://www.width.ai/post/what-is-jev-ai-typesafe), *via snippet*), and the paradox frames why model selection is getting more attention, not less. At fixed capability, prices fall about **10x per year** ([a16z](https://www.a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost)), or **9x to 900x per year depending on the threshold** ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)). Easy capabilities get cheap fastest, so each year a cheap tier can absorb more traffic. Frontier list prices have stayed in a band of roughly $2–5 input and $12–30 output per million tokens (*aggregator prices, conflicting*), so buyers of the best model see little deflation. Volume, meanwhile, grows faster than price falls. Google went from **9.7T tokens per month (May 2024) to 3.2 quadrillion (May 2026)** ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/149205/google-3-2-quadrillion-monthly-ai-tokens)). OpenAI's API went from 6B to 15B tokens per minute in about five months ([AI Daily Post](https://aidailypost.com/news/openai-api-token-usage-rises-from-6-bn-15-bn-per-minute-straining), *second-hand*). Nadella's "Jevons paradox strikes again!" (January 2025) became the industry's frame ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/01/27/microsoft-ceo-satya-nadella-deepseek-optimism-jevons-paradox)). Citadel Securities (August 2026) read usage-weighted token prices falling about 40% in seven weeks while GPU rents held and cloud revenue grew 43% as "tentative evidence" that Jevons dynamics dominate ([Citadel Securities](https://www.citadelsecurities.com/news-and-insights/global-macro-strategy/elastic-expectations/)). That is aggregate evidence, not causal. Capability gains drive demand too, and token counts include reasoning tokens. The only elasticity estimate found, about 1.42, comes from one unreviewed preprint ([arXiv 2601.12339](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12339v1)). Skeptics argue aggregate demand is inelastic and only cost-sensitive segments switch to cheap models.

For an application builder, Jevons shows up as cost per user and cost per task rising while price per token falls. Cost per task is price per token × tokens per task × attempts. Agents multiply the middle term 4–15x (Anthropic, above), and reasoning effort multiplies it again. Flat-rate products broke under this in 2025. Cursor moved Pro to usage-priced credits in June 2025 ([Lago](https://getlago.substack.com/p/why-every-ai-coding-tool-gets-pricing), *second-hand*). Anthropic added weekly Claude Code caps effective 28 August 2025 ([Slashdot](https://developers.slashdot.org/story/25/07/29/0156200/claude-code-users-hit-with-weekly-rate-limits)). Replit's gross margin swung between 36% and −14% during 2025 ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/replit/), *second-hand*). Artificial Analysis snippets put output tokens per task on the same index at roughly **27k to 119k across vendors' reasoning models**, a spread as large as the per-token price spread ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons), *unverified snippet figures*). Comparing models by list price per token therefore misleads, and the useful selection levers now include effort, agent depth and which sub-steps get a cheap model. The common claim that routing "matters more" as spend grows (40–60% of traffic is simple, 30–85% savings) comes from router vendors and practitioner blogs with a commercial interest ([digitalapplied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/llm-model-routing-2026-cost-quality-optimization-engineering-guide), *unsourced heuristic*). The defensible version: the share of tasks needing a frontier model shrinks, but the dollars at stake on that share grow.

## What the notebook's measurement must include to be believable

The literature's main lesson for a notebook comparing a cheap model, a strong model, a predictive router and a cascade is that a single "X% cheaper" number means nothing without its quality target and baseline. Every 2026 critique comes down to routers being compared against the wrong reference. The notebook should plot each strategy as a point, or a curve as its threshold is swept, on a **cost-per-task vs quality plane**, with these references on the same plot:

| Reference | Why it is needed |
|---|---|
| Always-cheap and always-strong | The two endpoints every strategy sits between |
| **Random mixing at equal cost** | The straight line between the endpoints. A router or cascade must sit above it at the same strong-call share, or it adds nothing. RouteLLM's best router was ~48% cheaper than random at equal quality ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)); AutoMix's simple thresholding sometimes fell below it |
| Best single model | The LLMRouterBench baseline that commercial routers failed to beat |
| Static task-type table | The cheap, inspectable rule a learned router must beat ([arXiv 2608.23023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23023)) |
| **Oracle ceiling** | The cheapest model that answers each item correctly. Report the fraction of the oracle gap each strategy recovers ([arXiv 2608.08265](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08265)) |

The router's own cost and latency belong in the totals: each Jev call's input tokens at $0.042/M, plus its 130–390 ms. The strong model's cost per task is not a fair denominator if the router's cost is left out. For the cascade, count the cheap call on every escalated item, any verifier or sampling calls, and the tail latency of cheap plus strong. Report p50 and p95 latency, not only means. Take cost from the **token counts each provider returns**, never from estimates: input, output, reasoning or thinking tokens (billed as output and invisible in the text), cache reads and writes, and retries. Price them at the rates in force on the run date, and record which models answered. The repo's `run_card` already does that.

Three further controls follow from the research. First, set the router's confidence threshold and the cascade's escalation threshold on a held-out split, then evaluate on a separate one. Calibration shifts out of distribution, and a threshold tuned on the test set flatters both. Second, treat the oracle as noisy. A single-draw oracle counts lucky samples as "routable" ([arXiv 2607.03436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03436)), so repeated runs, and the repo's journal of trials, should establish the spread before any gap is quoted. Third, pick a task where the cheap and strong models actually disagree, and make it a closed-answer task so correctness can be scored without a judge. Otherwise there is nothing to route, and the measurement shows only the price ratio. Following the repo's convention of showing the problem first, the strong model's bill on traffic that the cheap model handles correctly is the problem a reader should see before any routing appears.

## Conclusion

The naive "model router" is a real product category, but it is the least-evidenced way to control LLM cost. Where it works, most of its gain is coarse task type, which a static table captures. What has changed in 2026 is not the router's intelligence but its price. Decision models make a routing call cost at most about a hundredth of a cent, so the router no longer has to justify itself against its own overhead, only against the static table and random mixing. That moves the hard problem from "can we afford to decide?" to "can we trust the decision's confidence?", and the out-of-distribution calibration evidence says not yet without per-workload validation.

The Jevons framing points the other way from the naive model. Per-token selection matters less every year as cheap tiers absorb more tasks. Per-task selection matters more, because agents and reasoning multiply tokens faster than prices fall. The highest-value selection decisions now sit inside a task: which sub-agent gets which model, how hard to think, whether to loop again. Those are the same bounded, typed decisions that decision models were built for. A notebook that measures routing honestly, with real token counts, equal-cost random mixing and an oracle, will likely find that a cheap router recovers a small share of a large oracle gap. That finding is the lesson to show the reader, not a failure of the demo.
